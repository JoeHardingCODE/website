The Debt-onator

Perspective · 2026 · 6 min read

See I don't like debt, not in some fanantical Dave Ramsey manner whereby I deem it to be the return of the devil, but in a manner where I deem it to be a ball and chain. Debt interest is spending that does not directly purchase a public service or investment. It is a transfer to the holders of government debt. Borrowing is a modern feature of any government, and with that it is not inherently bad; but it simply consumes too much of national revenues.

The OBR forecasts that honouring debt is on track to rise to £137bn by the 2030's. If we simplify this down and take crude estimates that is equivalent to $300m a day.

I'll put this number into perspective. Zuckerberg's yacht cost 300 million, enough to employ 7142 nurses, and if we gave that to Farage he would have to declare 60 (0) 5 million donation declaration forms.

This is where the debate about the government finances begins to turn into point scoring and finger pointing.

This is not an uncommon sight, most developed nations around the world have high debt, but the UK's debt position is vulnerable and the argument is as follows. Inflation can reduce the real burden of fixed-rate nominal debt, but this fiscal benefit is weaker in the UK because a substantial share of government liabilities is index-linked. Which then leaves the government unable to artificially deteriorate the value of the bond over time, but also introduces volatility into government finances. Another reason follows from the QE years. While QE was active and the Bank Rate was near zero, the Asset Purchase Facility generated a net profit for the Treasury. Now, with the Bank Rate significantly higher, the Bank of England pays high interest on those commercial bank reserves while holding lower-yielding gilts. Beyond indexation and QE, a more conventional issue lies, where the lower rate gilts mature, only to be reissued by higher rate bonds. This will eventually convert 2% bond yields into 3.8-4.5% across various maturities, creating an upward pressure. This debt seems semi-permanent, any government that takes any meaningful steps to reducing the debt burden would get ousted at the earliest convenience. So what are the macroeconomic consequences?

Economic growth is normally an escape route out of debt, if you grow enough your tax receipts will be enough to cover this rising burden. This issue is the UK can not reliably grow at the rates required. If expenditures, that includes interest repayments, continue rising and the economy is not growing, then the 'fiscal headroom' former chancellor Rachel 'raved' about quickly vanishes, increasing the probability any one shock will result in more borrowing.

Yields on 10-year UK Gilt represent the basis for measuring risk-free assets in the lending market . As long as there is significant supply of sovereign paper due to the requirement to raise over £100 billion a year to meet net cash needs, then gilt yields remain high. This has resulted in setting an everlasting floor below yields of corporate bonds and lending rates of commercial banks, making capex very costly for firms, SME's and mortgage holders .

The rise in yields on gilts has affected liability driven investing (LDI) for UK corporate pension plans. Higher yields have helped in improving funding ratios for defined benefit schemes, but have resulted in diverting capital to sovereign debt and away from domestic private equity/venture investments.

Is it even remediable?

Eliminating the debt is a foolish premise, but how is it best to reduce the impact? Austerity is unpopular, and if it triggers a recession it could make the debt to gdp ratio worse. We could outgrow the debt. Supply side policies aimed at boosting productivity, labour market reforms, deregulation, investment etc can all boost growth. But governments have been trying to revive this economy for years, and any sort of embedded structural growth takes years to materialise. Another option is to restructure the debt, issue longer bonds during low interest rate periods, but this only stops it growing at the rate it already is.

Most economists agree that no policy works on its own. A sustainable solution typically requires a balanced approach:
Short Term: Capping discretionary spending and fixing high-volatility debt structures
Medium Term: Targeted, growth-oriented tax reforms to encourage private investment.
Long Term: Boosting baseline GDP growth through institutional and technological efficiency gains.
