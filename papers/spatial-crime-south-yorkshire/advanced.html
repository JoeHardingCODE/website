Spatial Clustering of Crime in South Yorkshire

The full technical treatment — model setup, diagnostics, heterogeneity, robustness and the case for and against the paper's conclusions.

Perspective·2026·12 min read

Model specification and the Spatial Durbin model

The Spatial Durbin Model sits at the top of the taxonomy here because it nests both the lag and error alternatives under testable restrictions. Setting the coefficient vector on the lagged covariates, θ, to zero collapses SDM back to SAR, and the paper tests that restriction directly with a likelihood ratio test rather than assuming it. What it doesn't test explicitly is the common factor restriction θ = −ρβ that would collapse SDM into SEM, so the case for lag over error rests more on the earlier LM battery and the offender mobility story than on a formal nested test against SEM. This matters because SDM coefficients on their own don't mean much. A covariate enters twice, once through its own area effect and once through its spatially lagged version, and the two interact through the (I − ρW)⁻¹ multiplier before you get anything resembling a marginal effect. There's also a structural circularity worth sitting with: crime in one LSOA is modelled as partly a function of crime in its neighbours, which are themselves partly a function of crime in the first LSOA. The model resolves this through matrix inversion rather than any causal ordering, which is standard practice, but it means ρ is doing double duty as both a spillover parameter and an implicit assumption about how quickly that inversion converges, since it requires ρ to stay below the inverse of the largest eigenvalue of W, something row standardisation conveniently guarantees.

Data, deprivation controls and the collinearity problem

The decision to use domain ranks rather than the composite IMD score is really about avoiding a mechanical form of endogeneity: the composite score already has a crime domain baked into it, so regressing crime on a variable that partly contains crime would inflate fit for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying relationship. The collinearity problem that follows is more interesting than it first looks. Income, employment and education deprivation correlate at 0.93 to 0.96 with each other at LSOA level, which isn't surprising given how IMD domains are constructed, but averaging the three standardised domains into one index is a fairly blunt fix. It assumes each domain contributes equally to the underlying construct, an assumption that principal components analysis would relax by letting the data assign the weights, at the cost of losing the clean one standard deviation interpretation the paper relies on later. The tradeoff is stated plainly in the paper: the composite solves collinearity but destroys the ability to say which domain is driving the crime relationship. That's not a minor cost. If income deprivation and employment deprivation operate through different mechanisms, material hardship against social exclusion, say, collapsing them into one index means the coefficient reported is some unknown weighted average of two potentially distinct channels, and there's no way to recover the individual domain effects from the composite specification alone.

Diagnostics: Moran's I, Lagrange Multiplier tests and model selection

Moran's I on its own can tell you spatial dependence exists but says nothing about its form, which is why the LM battery matters more than the headline test. The clever part of the Anselin decision rule is the robust versions of LM-lag and LM-error, which strip out the score contribution attributable to the other process before testing. Without that adjustment, a strong lag process will often make the error test look significant too, purely because the two null hypotheses share information under misspecification, and you end up unable to tell them apart. Here the robust LM-lag statistic clears its threshold while robust LM-error doesn't, which is the textbook signal to move toward a lag specification. Worth noting though: these tests are only powered to detect deviation toward SAR or SEM specifically, not toward SDM. The paper's later LR test comparing SDM against SAR is doing separate work, testing a different and more general alternative that the LM battery was never built to catch. Combining the two forms of evidence, the LM sequence pointing to lag over error and the LR test pointing to SDM over SAR, is a reasonable way to build the case, but it's worth being clear that these are two different testing frameworks answering two different questions rather than one continuous chain of logic.

Direct, indirect and total effects under SAR and SDM

The spatial multiplier (I − ρW)⁻¹ expands into I + ρW + ρ²W² and so on, so the indirect effect reported in the impacts table is the sum of influence propagating through second and third order neighbours, decaying geometrically with each step. In SDM this gets more complicated again because the indirect effect has two separate sources feeding into it: the θ coefficient on the spatially lagged covariate itself, and the ρ-driven feedback of the lagged dependent variable. The impacts table reports a single indirect number, but that number is a blend of two mechanistically different spillover channels, and there's no way to split the blend back into its parts from the summary table alone. The population density result makes this concrete. A negative direct effect paired with a positive indirect one under SDM isn't something a SAR model could ever produce, because SAR only has one channel for spillover. Seeing the sign flip between direct and indirect for density, and not for deprivation or tenure, is decent evidence that whatever process links density to crime spillover differs in structure from the other two covariates, operating through the covariate channel rather than the outcome channel. None of this comes from the raw coefficient table. You only see it once you run the simulation based inference needed to get standard errors on these nonlinear functions of the parameters, since the delta method gets awkward once ρ and θ start interacting inside a matrix inverse.

Spatial heterogeneity: regimes, GWR and influential observations

Holding ρ fixed across local authorities while letting the own area coefficients vary assumes the underlying spatial process, offenders and victims moving across physical boundaries, works the same way everywhere in the county even if the strength of the deprivation or tenure relationship differs locally. That's defensible given the theory in the paper, offender mobility doesn't know about council boundaries, and a model that let ρ vary too would be answering a different question. GWR is a local smoothing technique rather than a properly specified spatial econometric model: it doesn't address the endogeneity built into Wy, and local windows with only a few hundred observations can produce unstable coefficients purely from small sample noise, which is exactly why a permutation based Monte Carlo test on the variance of the local coefficients is used to check whether apparent local variation is systematic rather than random noise. The influence diagnostics raise a separate concern. The DFBETAS screening was run on the non-spatial OLS specification rather than the SDM, so strictly it identifies which LSOAs are influential for the non-spatial tenure relationship, not necessarily the spatial one. What makes the finding useful anyway is what happens when you remove the flagged LSOAs: residual autocorrelation reappears. That's a stronger piece of evidence than the DFBETAS score itself, since it shows those observations were doing work in the spatial structure rather than just being large residuals sitting on top of an otherwise fitting model.

Robustness to the spatial weights matrix

There's a foundational weak point in the entire SAR and SDM framework that the robustness section only partly addresses: W is treated as known and exogenous, chosen by the researcher rather than estimated from the data, and every downstream inference is conditional on that choice being correct. Testing across queen contiguity, rook contiguity, k = 5 nearest neighbours and inverse distance weighting is a sensible way to check whether the substantive results survive different definitions of adjacency, and here they mostly do, with the deprivation coefficient staying in a tight band across all four. But it's worth noticing that all four specifications are still geography based measures of physical closeness. None of them test a properly different theoretical channel, commuting patterns or road network distance, for instance, which would be a harder test of whether the offender mobility story holds up against a different notion of what makes two areas neighbours. The inverse distance case is the interesting outlier: residual autocorrelation persists there while it doesn't under the other three, and the likely explanation is that inverse distance weighting creates a much denser matrix, an average of ninety neighbours against five or six for the others, which dilutes local dependence with a lot of weak, long range connections that don't map onto any real mechanism of crime spillover.
