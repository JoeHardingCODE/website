Spatial Clustering of Crime in South Yorkshire

A deeper explanation of the same paper, still written to be readable.

Perspective·2026·9 min read

Spatial dependence and why OLS falls short

The first challenge arises due to the fact that crime does not stay within their LSOA boundaries. Criminals roam, victims/targets group, and displacement effect from policing intensity results in the movement of criminal activity in to adjacent streets, which means that, when one regresses crime on deprivation and demographic factors while ignoring location, one assumes implicitly that errors in all LSOAs are not related to errors in neighboring LSOAs, which is most likely incorrect. This assumption is confirmed empirically in the paper: Global Moran's I equal to 0.406 for log crime rate reveals substantial clustering of crime rate and, more importantly for the OLS model critique, the residuals from the base model (which includes only crime rates as an independent variable) reveal autocorrelation even after adjustment for deprivation, density, and tenure. It is the second result that decreases OLS' credibility: it implies that spatial effects are not an artifact of missing deprivation variables, but persist even after control for them.

Choosing between a spatial lag and a spatial error model

Now that it's known that we need a spatial model, the remaining question is which mechanism should be modeled: does the crime rate in an LSOA influence the crime rate in the neighboring LSOAs (lag process), or is the source of correlation in the errors a combination of other, unobservable spatially clustered effects, such as policing density. Following Anselin's typical procedure, one can perform Lagrange Multiplier tests on the OLS residuals and check the robust versions after controlling for the other effect, and in the present case, both LM error and LM lag are significant, but only the latter is significant after accounting for error dependence, suggesting a lag process. A likelihood ratio test is used against the Spatial Durbin Model that nests the SAR model under the restriction that the lagged covariates sum up to zero. This restriction is soundly rejected, so the model selected is the SDM model rather than SAR, which is a reasonable model choice, though it should be noted that this is following LeSage and Pace's "general-to-specific" approach rather than LM testing sequence, which are not nested tests.

Direct, indirect and total effects

This is where the SAR/SDM apparatus proves itself. Given that the dependent variable influences itself through ρW, an increase in deprivation within a LSOA has not only an effect on its own crime rate but affects neighboring LSOAs, too, and then neighbors in return influence our LSOA. Hence, the value of β for the variable deprivation is not a marginal effect in any meaningful manner. The decomposition of the coefficient into three categories provided by LeSage and Pace, including direct, indirect and total effect, is the right interpretation for such coefficients. It is also a reasonable solution to the interpretational problem: because the deprivation index is a standardized index in which 1 SD equals approximately 9,000-10,000 national rank points, it measures the impact in terms of 100 rank points, resulting in an overall impact of 0.46% for both SAR and SDM. The private rents ratio prevails in percentage terms (an increase of 10pp translates into an increase of crime rates of 21-30%), whereas population density is by far the most informative outcome: while SAR reveals negative direct and indirect effects that reinforce each other, SDM separates the two and finds negative direct (−7.46%) and positive indirect (+5.98%) effects, which result in a fairly small insignificant effect. The difference between SAR and SDM with regard to the population density measure can be regarded as the main result of the study.

Does one equation fit all of South Yorkshire?

Having determined the significance of space, this paper wonders about whether one can use one equation for the four places (Sheffield, Barnsley, Doncaster, and Rotherham) to explain the relationships in question or whether there is a variation in such relationship across the areas. To this end, this paper performs such a comparison through a three-fold analysis. First, it uses the nested nested LR test by allowing the own-area and the lag coefficients (Regimes A and B respectively) to vary by local authority. None of the comparisons reject the pooled equation (all p > 0.25). However, AIC increases significantly as more regimes are allowed (up to 1017.9 as compared to 1000.0 for the pooled equation). Secondly, the geographically weighted regression approach shows better fit in general terms; however, permutation test (Monte Carlo) only detects non-stationarity of population density while deprivation barely misses and tenure does not feature at all. Thirdly, and this is the point of interest of this analysis, it emerges that three non-residential city-centre LSOAs (two in Sheffield and one in Doncaster) exert undue influence on the coefficient of tenure in particular. The dummy variable drops AIC considerably but reintroduces significant residual spatial autocorrelation that had previously been absorbed.

What the paper doesn't (yet) show

This is a cross-sectional and ecological study; no such study can draw inferences about individuals or causality, and the standard caution against the ecological fallacy applies. LSOAs are administrative units, so findings may depend on which areal unit is chosen. The denominator of residents is bound to inflate rates in some high-visitor LSOAs, which may account for the extreme skew in the untransformed dependent variable and for the density finding; an ambient population-adjusted rate, or sensitivity analysis that excludes the LSOA with the most extreme value, would be a natural follow up step for later research. Recorded crime is as much affected by practices of reporting and recording as it is by offending, and reporting rates are likely to vary depending on deprivation levels, something that the data cannot reveal. Pooling three IMD domains into a single composite solved the problem of collinearity but lost the information on the separate domains. The proportion of private rented dwellings does not differentiate between HMOs, student dwellings, short and long term lettings, each likely to have its own relationship with crime in the area. For instance the lowest ranking area, E01007862, and least deprived area is student accommodation that would fall under rented tenure. Assuming this to have the same relationship with crime is, as I see it, is a strong assumption that requires further testing. Future work should look towards establishing a causal relationship and making amends to the faults listed above. (As read in the paper)
